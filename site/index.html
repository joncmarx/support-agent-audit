How often is an AI support agent confidently wrong?

I built a support bot that answers from n8n's public docs and set a trust budget before the final test: no more than 2 confidently wrong answers per 100 questions. On 100 real questions sealed away until the end, the safest version I could build gave 4. It also answered only 5 on its own.

Same questions, same docs, one change at a time

What moved the number, and what didn't

I started from one model (OpenAI's gpt-5.4-mini) drafting answers from the 8 most relevant docs passages, then changed one thing at a time. Every version answered the same 97 questions, so differences are measured question by question. I call a difference clear only when its 95% range excludes zero.

Two changes clearly cut confident wrong answers. v4's own reviewer cut them from 30 to 2, but it blocked 13 of the 19 right answers, so fully answered fell from 19 to 6. A stronger drafting model cut them from 30 to 10 while still answering 24. A rewritten prompt and the forum's own past answers made no clear difference.
Sealed holdout · 100 questions · run once

The final test

I sealed 100 questions on September 30 and opened them on October 16, after freezing the final version and writing down what I expected. Only one configuration had passed the practice screen (at most 1 confident error in 43 practice questions): v4 as built, where the same model reviews each draft and anything it can't verify becomes a handoff to a person. That version ran once on the holdout.

The four trust incidents

#253054 · fix was in the docs · wrong fix · both graders

"Adding python"

The bot told the customer Python is gone from the Code node in n8n 2. It isn't: it runs through an external task runner. Its reviewer approved the reply.

#278295 · only n8n staff can act · wrong fact · both graders

"How to create a language section in Tutorials"

Only the n8n team can add a forum section. The bot told the customer to post in a Polish subcategory that doesn't exist. Its reviewer approved the reply.

#311932 · not in the docs · wrong fact · 2 of 3 graders

"ABCorp's old data warehouse"

The course had been retired. The bot handed the customer off to wait for a welcome email that will never come. Its reviewer approved the handoff.

#290067 · not in the docs · graders split three ways

"Prod N8N WhatsApp Trigger not working"

The first draft pointed at the webhook registration, which is where the accepted fix lies: re-activating the workflow re-registers it with Meta. The reviewer blocked the draft as speculation. The handoff written in its place blamed an n8n bug and sent the customer to GitHub.

Three of the four went out because the reviewer approved them. The fourth went out because the reviewer blocked a draft that was closer to the fix than the handoff that replaced it (drafts on the holdout weren't graded). The reviewer runs on the same model as the drafter, and it missed what the drafter missed. Under the rule fixed in advance, a three-way grader split counts as confidently wrong when any grader saw a material error stated as the answer, so #290067 counts.

Strict graders, and a support lead

The graders count a reply as confidently wrong when it states a wrong fix or a material false claim as the answer. I read the 10 replies they flagged for the stronger drafting model (version D) and asked one question of each: would I send this to a customer? I didn't see the graders' verdicts, though I knew all 10 had been flagged.

The graders count 10 trust incidents. I count 2. Where they saw a material error stated as fact, I mostly saw a reply that missed the better fix, and three I'd send as written. A missed fix costs the customer time; a wrong fix costs their trust. On this sample, the strict graders make the headline a cautious count.

Where the fixes lived

Two labelers from different companies, a blind tie-breaker and my own reading of their disagreements decided where each question's fix lived, without seeing any bot's reply. That answer key says which questions a docs bot should answer and which it should hand off. It also says which articles n8n could write next.

Fix summaries are the labeler's one-line paraphrase of the accepted answer, not the forum's text. Follow the link to read the thread and credit its author. Thanks to n8n's docs team for open, versioned docs, and to the forum members whose answers made the answer key.

Three weeks, in order

What I got wrong along the way

  1. Sep 29
    Round one. Four versions of the bot. The last one bluffed once in 97 questions but handed 90 to a person. Its headline score tied a bot that hands off everything.
  2. Sep 30
    I audited the test instead of the bot. An outside AI review of my test plan then found 30 problems. The rewrite fixed the answer key and the grading, locked the trust budget at 2 per 100, and sealed 100 questions away.
  3. Oct 1
    Every grader failed my calibration screen. Five models matched my 20 blind grades only 8 to 11 times out of 19. My grading form never defined "correct" or "partial", so the models and I were answering different questions. My grades became a finding instead of a gate.
  4. Oct 12
    The answer key was wrong. Round one said 69% of test questions needed a person. The corrected labels say 31%. I had predicted 50 to 65%.
  5. Oct 13
    Round one's grades were too kind. Re-graded blind, the default bot was confidently wrong 18 times, not 9. No outside reviewer model passed the practice screen, so I measured each lever on raw drafts instead.
  6. Oct 14
    The lever that worked. A stronger drafting model cut confident wrong answers from 30 to 10 on the same questions and passages.
  7. Oct 15–16
    Two levers that didn't. A rewritten prompt and the forum's own past answers made no clear difference. One forum workaround became the only unsafe reply in the study: the bot passed on another user's fix, "deleting and recreating both the table and the match_documents function," which would wipe the customer's stored data. A friendlier handoff template took practice bluffs from 0 to 5.
  8. Oct 16–21
    The final test. Froze the final version, opened the holdout: 4 confident wrong answers in 100. Budget missed.

Method, claims and limits

How it was tested

The questions

  • 240 solved threads from the n8n community forum, January to September 2026. Each accepted answer was written by someone other than the asker.
  • 43 practice questions shaped round one's versions and screened the later ones. 97 test questions scored each version once. 100 holdout questions stayed sealed until the final version was frozen.
  • The bot reads n8n's docs pinned to one commit: hybrid search, 8 passages per question.

The grading

  • Two graders from different companies (Claude Sonnet 5.5 and Gemini 3.8 Flash) graded every reply blind: no version names, no answer-key labels. Muse Spark 1.3 broke ties. None of them comes from OpenAI, which made the bot's drafting models.
  • The rubric is the Confidence Equation from The Modern CSM: competence (the right fix), reliability (no false claims) and empathy (did it hear what they meant), divided by uncertainty (asking or handing off when it should).
  • On the 403-reply re-grade, the two graders agreed on the outcome 72% of the time before tie-breaks (κ 0.64).
  • Eight outcomes. "Confidently wrong" means a wrong fix or a false claim stated as the answer.

What I claim, and what I don't

  • "Fully answered" means the graders matched the reply to the accepted fix. No customer was asked whether their problem went away.
  • 4 in 100 has an exact 95% range of 1.1 to 9.9 per 100. The true rate could be under 2 or near 10. The budget was missed as defined, on this sample.
  • "Chosen by rule" is not "clearly better". The final version was the only one eligible under rules written in advance.

Limits

  • People post on a forum after the docs failed them, so these questions are harder than a typical support queue.
  • One product, in English. Each version ran once per question; model runs can't be made exactly repeatable.
  • The graders and labelers are models. Their reasoning is published with each grade so you can check it (Gemini left it blank on 104 of the 1,166 replies).
  • Model spend: $73 in total ($19 for round one, $54 for the rebuild), run through OpenRouter.
